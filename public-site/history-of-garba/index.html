HISTORY OF GARBA

A tradition that survives by changing.

Garba is rooted in ritual and devotion, but every generation reshapes its music, dress and performance style.

01 · RITUAL ROOTS

The centre came before the spectacle.

UNESCO describes Garba as a ritualistic and devotional dance performed during Navratri, around a lit earthenware pot or an image of the mother goddess Amba. The circular movement, singing and clapping place devotion and collective participation at the centre of the form.

This is the safest way to understand Garba's history. Its identity is not built around one singer, one choreography or one modern event format. It is a community practice with a ritual centre.

02 · ACROSS GENERATIONS

Garba is learned by taking part.

UNESCO notes that Garba is transmitted in both rural and urban areas through practice, performance, imitation and observation. Families, dancers, musicians, craftspeople, organisers, religious figures, schools and cultural organisations all participate in keeping the tradition active.

That matters because Garba has not survived as a museum piece. It survives because people continue to perform it.

03 · CHANGE

New styles do not automatically make Garba less traditional.

In its 2023 decision, UNESCO explicitly noted that every generation redefines Garba through new performing styles, lyrics and dress while retaining the circle of feminine energy, or Shakti. That gives the tradition room to evolve without losing its cultural centre.

Modern amplification, large grounds, recorded music, new arrangements and changing fashion sit alongside older community practices. The useful distinction is not simply old versus new. It is whether the cultural structure and meaning remain legible.

04 · RECOGNITION

Garba of Gujarat entered UNESCO's Representative List in 2023.

The inscription recognised Garba as living intangible cultural heritage and highlighted its connection to music, costume-making, pottery, dance and community participation.
